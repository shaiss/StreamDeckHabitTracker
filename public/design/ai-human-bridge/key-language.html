The OLED Key Language

A living guide · the AI ⇄ Human Bridge design study, rendered

A Stream Deck-like device — a small grid of glowing, labelled, physically pressable keys — is a surprisingly good surface for supervising an AI agent. But if every key looks different, the human has to read the deck like a paragraph every time. This guide defines the shared visual grammar that turns the deck into a glanceable decision surface, and renders it live. Each key is a semantic tile that answers — in under a second, the same way every time — four questions:

QuestionChannel that answers it
Whose turn is it? (AI working / waiting on me / idle)the frame — color + motion
What is this?the glyph (icon) + label
What happens if I press?the glyph's verb + affordance
How urgent?motion rate + brightness
Two accessibility checks from the study (docs 2.2 & 5.2).

01 Overview the thesis · doc 1

AI is moving from chat to supervision. When an agent runs work in the background — a coding task, a research sweep, a coach reasoning about your habits — the human's job stops being "author every prompt" and becomes "make the few decisions the AI can't make alone." A text box is a poor instrument for that job: it demands foreground focus, it's serial, and every approval is a modal interruption.

Why the chat box is the wrong tool for supervision

  • It demands foreground focus. To answer, you context-switch your whole screen back into the conversation.
  • It's serial and modal. You can't glance at "three agents are waiting" — you deal with them one blocking dialog at a time.
  • It has no ambient state. Between messages, the AI's status is invisible. Is it working? Stuck? Done? You have to go look.
  • Every decision costs a sentence. "Yes, approved" is a lot of ceremony for a binary your thumb could answer.

The result is approval fatigue and supervision blindness.

Why a grid of keys fits

  • Peripheral & ambient. A mostly-dark deck with one amber key pulsing is a complete status report you read without turning your head.
  • Colocated I/O. The screen (AI → human) and the button (human → AI) are the same physical object. You press the thing that's asking.
  • Physical = committal. A press is a deliberate, embodied act — exactly right for a decision, and it lets dangerous actions feel dangerous.
  • Spatially addressable → muscle memory. "Approve" is always in the same place; over days your thumb answers without reading.
  • Bounded — and that's the point. Fifteen keys can't hold a conversation, so the AI can't dump everything on you. Scarcity of keys is scarcity of interruptions.

The Stream Deck turns the human into a bank of fast, physical I/O ports the AI can call — a decision surface for supervising agents, without pulling the person back into a chat window.

Where the deck is the wrong tool

A study that only lists strengths is marketing. The deck is a poor fit for long-form or nuanced input (that stays in chat), for reading detail (a 72px key labels a decision but can't show the diff — it should route to detail), for high-volume streams (if the AI needs a human 200×/hour, the autonomy is broken), and for rare one-off tasks (muscle memory accrues over repeated supervision). The honest framing: the deck is the fast path for the common, bounded decisions of ongoing supervision, sitting beside the screen and the chat.

02 The aesthetic ground Nocturne Ritual · doc 2.2

The study builds on an aesthetic already in this repo: Nocturne Ritual. The deck spends most of its life looking like it, so the language had to reconcile with it rather than replace it. Three ideas form the ground:

  • A deep-night canvas. Near-black backgrounds, dim chrome, negative space as a design element. A mostly-dark deck is not empty — it's quiet, and quiet is information.
  • The rounded square. Every key is the same rounded-rect tile. Uniformity of shape is what lets color and motion carry meaning instead.
  • Color as identity. Each ritual object carries one hue, derived from its name and kept for life — hue is memory.

The one sharp disagreement — and its resolution

Here is the study's sharpest disagreement with the aesthetic, named head-on. Nocturne Ritual says color belongs to identity. This study instead spends color on state. Both cannot own the frame. The resolution is a division of the key's real estate, not a winner:

  • State owns the frame; object identity owns the interior. The "one hue, kept for life" rule moves inward — it colors the glyph and its halo — while the frame ring is reserved for turn-state. A habit key at rest is exactly the Ritual key you already ship; only when the AI needs a decision does the frame light amber.
  • Violet stays sacred. Violet means "the coach is speaking." The study honors that by putting agent identity in the status dot, never competing with the amber frame. One says who, the other says whose turn.
  • The Ritual palette is the ground; the state palette is the signal. Everything calm and identity-colored is the resting deck. The six state colors appear only when a human is needed — brief, purposeful, then gone.

State owns the frame, object identity owns the glyph, agent identity owns the dot. Three zones, three jobs, no collision — that firewall is the whole reconciliation.

03 The language doc 2 — the grammar

The organizing principle: one signal, one meaning, everywhere. A color means exactly one thing. A motion means exactly one thing. A zone holds exactly one kind of information. Consistency is the entire value; a "clever" exception destroys glanceability.

Color is never the only signal. Roughly 1 in 12 people has a color-vision deficiency, so every state is redundantly encoded: wait = amber + breathing; working = blue + sweep/shimmer; blocked = red + fast blink + a ! glyph; success = green + a check + settle-and-fade.

3.3 · The motion vocabulary doc 2.3 — calm by default

These are screens, not static keycaps, so motion is a first-class token — often more glanceable than color. The governing rule: calm by default; loud only when earned. At most one "loud" (fast) key at a time; everything else is still.

MotionMeaningFeel
Solid / stilla settled state (idle, done)the default; silence is information

The press-acknowledgment flash matters more than it looks: it closes the loop. The flash is the deck saying "got it" — then the frame changes state to show what your press did. Motion honors a global "reduced motion" preference by falling back to brightness steps (try the toggle above).

3.4 · Typography doc 2.4 — ruthless at 72px

A key is 72–120px and read from 2–3 feet away. Type is ruthless:

  • One word if possible, two at most. APPROVE, RUN TESTS, HANDOFF.
  • Uppercase monospace for labels. Mono is the authentic voice of a control surface; uppercase buys legibility at small sizes.
  • Numbers are badges, not words. "3 pending" becomes a 3 badge. Digits never fight the label for the same row.
  • Contrast is non-negotiable. Label text sits at the top of the contrast range in every state.

The mono / sans split is the type system: mono is the device's voice, sans is the app's voice.

3.5 · The glyph grammar doc 2.5 — one verb, one glyph, everywhere

Glyphs name the verb — what pressing does — so the affordance is legible before the label is read. Filled means active/running; outline means available. A small, teachable set.

04 Interaction patterns doc 3 — composed from the tokens, nothing new

These are the "components" of the language: named, learn-once shapes for the recurring moments of supervision. Composing patterns

Real supervision is these patterns layered over time on the same grid. A Status Board sits idle across the top; a build finishes and its tile flips to an Approval Gate ("deploy?"). You press DETAILS, the plan routes to your screen, you press APPROVE (two-stage, because it's prod). The tile flashes, settles to working blue, then success green, then fades to idle. None of that required reading a manual — each new pattern was recognizable the first time because it was built from signals you already knew.

05 Layout & protocol doc 4 — the grid and the state machine

5.1 · The grid grammar

A 5×3 deck (15 keys) is the reference; the conventions scale. The core idea mirrors a phone's UI chrome: stable zones, so where a thing lives tells you what kind of thing it is.

┌─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┐
│  STATUS ROW — ambient, non-interactive / drill-in   │   top row
├─────┼─────┼─────┼─────┼─────┤
│  ACTION ZONE — the current decision(s) live here    │   middle rows
├─────┼─────┼─────┼─────┼─────┤
│ ‹BACK│     │     │     │MENU▤│   nav row (anchors fixed at the corners)
└─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┘

Top = status board · middle = the decision that needs you · bottom corners = fixed BACK (left) and MENU (right) anchors your thumb hits blind.

  • The focus convention. A decision is staged in the same region every time (center of the action zone) rather than wherever there's a free key. Predictable location beats optimal packing.
  • Paging & progressive disclosure. Breadth stays shallow; depth comes from pages. A ⊕ drill-in swaps the grid to a sub-context, always with BACK in its corner.
  • Off is part of the layout. Unused keys stay dark (OLED truly off). Negative space is a design element — filling every key "because it's there" destroys the signal. Darkness is how the deck says nothing here.

The AI drives state and pages within its surface; it never seizes the human's context. Paging is an invitation the deck extends, not a place it drags you. (This repo's docs/DECK-UX.md already drew that consent boundary: the coach may raise its voice in the room it's in, never walk into another.)

5.2 · The turn-taking state machine

Under the visuals, each key is a small state machine. The value of the whole system is that these states look identical on every key — document 2's frame colors and motions are the rendering of these states.

       ┌──────────────────────────── AI updates ────────────────────────────┐
       ▼                                                                      │
    ┌──────┐  AI starts work   ┌─────────┐  AI needs human   ┌──────────┐    │
    │ IDLE │ ────────────────► │ WORKING │ ────────────────► │   WAIT   │    │
    └──────┘                   └─────────┘                   └──────────┘    │
       ▲                            │                          │    │        │
       │                            │ fails                    │    │ press  │
       │                            ▼                          │    ▼        │
       │                       ┌─────────┐                     │ ┌──────────┐│
       │◄──── fade (timeout) ──│ BLOCKED │◄── press: retry ────┘ │CONFIRMING││
       │                       └─────────┘                       └──────────┘│
       │                                                              │       │
    ┌──────┐                                                          │ result│
    │ DONE │◄─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘◄─────┘
    └──────┘  (transient — settles, then fades to IDLE)

The critical transition is WORKING → WAIT: the AI yields the turn. That's the moment the deck lights amber and the human is called. WAIT → CONFIRMING is the human taking the turn back (the acknowledgment flash). Turn-taking made physical and visible.

5.3 · The per-key data model

The study is about the language, not an implementation, but it should be buildable. The data model per key is deliberately tiny:

{
  "id": "deploy-gate",
  "glyph": "check",          // verb → glyph (doc 2.5)
  "label": "APPROVE",        // ≤ 2 words
  "state": "wait",           // idle | working | wait | confirming | done | blocked
  "badge": null,             // number, or null
  "agent": "builder",        // → identity dot hue
  "danger": true,            // arms two-stage confirm (3.8)
  "action": "approve:deploy" // token returned to the AI on the committing press
}

state is what the AI emits. The two-stage armed window is a deck-local substate the bridge tracks for danger keys — it isn't an AI-emitted value, so the enum stays six values.

This maps cleanly onto mechanisms agents already have — tool-use permission prompts, human-in-the-loop checkpoints, and the elicitation step where an agent asks a bounded question. The deck is, precisely, a physical renderer for human-in-the-loop requests: anywhere an agent today pops a "can I do this?" dialog, that request can render as a key instead.

06 Principles & open questions doc 5

The ten principles

The north stars the rest of the study derives from. When two design choices conflict, these break the tie.

  1. Glanceability over completeness. A key must be read in under a second from across the desk.
  2. State before content. Whose turn it is dominates the key.
  3. One signal, one meaning. Consistency is the entire value; clever exceptions are bugs.
  4. Calm by default, loud only when earned. At most one loud key at a time.
  5. Physical means committal. Reserve presses for decisions; make irreversible ones feel irreversible.
  6. Spatial consistency. The same action lives in the same place, forever. Muscle memory is the superpower.
  7. Constraint is a feature. Few keys force the AI to surface only what truly needs a human.
  8. Legible when dark. An off key is a valid, meaningful state.
  9. Reversible and forgiving. BACK is always in its corner; destructive actions are always two-stage.
  10. Progressive disclosure. Depth via paging; breadth stays shallow.

Open questions — what a prototype must answer

This is a study, so the honest deliverable is not just a design but a list of the things the design doesn't yet know.

  • Legibility & the physical limit. How much can a 72px key actually carry? At what distance does it break? Is the verb set recognizable without a legend after a day?
  • Attention economics. How loud is too loud? Does an always-present amber pulse stop being noticed over weeks (habituation)? Does ambient peripheral display actually beat on-screen notifications?
  • Expressiveness vs. simplicity. Do the nine patterns cover real agent supervision, or does every domain sprout bespoke layouts? Which bound — six states, six colors, six motions — runs out first?
  • Scale. What happens at 20 agents? Does the grammar degrade gracefully on a 2×3 (6-key) deck?
  • The physical loop. How fast is press → response → visible result? What's the fallback when a slow result leaves a key mid-animation?
  • Accessibility (must, not nice-to-have). Does the redundant motion/glyph encoding carry every state with color removed? Does the reduced-motion fallback preserve every distinction?

The prototype is already half-built. Habit Tracker already drives live key faces, already runs a reactive coach, already has an "Ask" takeover and a nudge/consent model. The cheapest experiment that would teach the most: adopt the frame-vs-interior split — keep each habit's Ritual hue on the glyph, let the frame ring go amber-breathing only when the coach's "Ask" is pending — add one Attention Beacon, and live with it for a week. If the amber frame earns its place, the rest is worth building out.

This is a rendering, not the product. It exists so the grammar in the study can be seen and felt instead of imagined. The study's open questions — how much a 72px key really carries, how loud is too loud, whether the states survive real colorblindness testing — are exactly what a prototype on this repo's real key faces is positioned to answer.
